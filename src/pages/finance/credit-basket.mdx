# A flat distribution of defaults

Take a basket of $n$ credits. Each one defaults with the same probability $p$,
and every pair of them has the same asset correlation $\rho$. How many defaults
should you expect, and how are they distributed?

Two cases are easy. If the names are independent, the count is binomial. If they
are perfectly correlated they share one fate: either none default or all of them
do. Everything between those two needs numerical work.

Except at one point, where the answer is as simple as an answer can be.

## The model

This is the Gaussian copula, the standard model for a credit basket. Each name
has a latent asset return, and the name defaults when that return falls below a
threshold. Matching the threshold to the default probability $p$ puts it at
$\Phi^{-1}(p)$ standard deviations.

Correlation enters through a single market factor $M$:

$$
R_k = \sqrt{\rho}\, M + \sqrt{1-\rho}\, Z_k
$$

where $Z_k$ is noise particular to name $k$. Conditional on the market — once $M$
is known — the names are independent, so the number of defaults is binomial with
a shifted probability. Integrating that binomial over the market factor gives
the distribution:

$$
p(m) = \Phi\!\left( \frac{\Phi^{-1}(p) - m\sqrt{\rho}}{\sqrt{1-\rho}} \right)
$$

$$
\Pr[N = j] = \binom{n}{j} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(m)^{j}\,\bigl(1 - p(m)\bigr)^{n-j} \varphi(m)\, dm
$$

That integral normally wants a quadrature rule. Which is the point: closed-form
answers are scarce here, and scarce enough that a single one is worth having,
if only to check the numerical methods against.

## The special case

Look at $p(m)$. It would be much friendlier if $\Phi^{-1}(p)$ were zero — that
is, if $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$. And friendlier still if the $\sqrt{\rho}$ and
$\sqrt{1-\rho}$ matched, which happens when $\rho = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Put both
together and the conditional probability collapses to

$$
p(m) = \Phi(-m) = 1 - \Phi(m)
$$

Substituting $u = \Phi(m)$ turns the integral into a beta integral, and out
comes $B(n-j+1,\, j+1)$. But there is a better route that needs no calculus at
all.

With $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $\rho = \tfrac{1}{2}$ the factor model is just

$$
R_k = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}}\,(M + Z_k)
$$

and name $k$ defaults when $R_k < 0$ — equivalently when $M < -Z_k$. Write
$M^{*} = -M$, and name $k$ defaults exactly when $M^{*} > Z_k$.

So the number of defaults is *the number of the $Z$'s that $M^{*}$ exceeds*.
Sort $M^{*}$ and the $n$ noise terms into one list. They are identically
distributed continuous random variables, so $M^{*}$ is equally likely to land in
any of the $n+1$ positions. Therefore

$$
\Pr[N = j] = \frac{1}{n+1}
$$

Every outcome — no defaults, one default, all of them — is equally likely. The
distribution is flat, and it stays flat for any $n$.

## What to look for

**The mean never moves.** Slide the correlation from zero to one and the
expected number of defaults stays at $n p$ throughout. Correlation does not
change how likely any individual name is to default; it changes only how much
they default *together*. Everything correlation does, it does to the shape.

**The tails are what correlation buys.** At low correlation the distribution
concentrates near the mean and the extremes are negligible. Raise it and
probability drains out of the middle into both ends — many defaults at once
becomes far more likely, and so does none at all. This is the whole reason
correlation matters for a tranche: a senior tranche only takes losses in the
right tail, which barely exists at low correlation and is substantial at high.

**Flat is not the same as random.** A uniform distribution over $0 \ldots n$ defaults
looks like the least informative outcome possible, but it comes from a very
specific pair of parameters. Move either one and the flatness goes.

## Notes

The distribution is computed by numerical integration over the market factor,
using a composite Gauss-Legendre rule with panels placed on the transition
region — as $\rho$ approaches 1 the conditional default probability becomes
nearly a step function in $m$, and a fixed grid misses it. At
$p = \rho = \tfrac{1}{2}$ the computed values agree with $1/(n+1)$ to machine
precision, which is the tightest
check available: the code has to reproduce an exact identity, not merely come
close.

This page follows Pete Benson, "Distribution of Defaults in a Credit Basket: An
Interesting Special Case", _RiskMetrics Journal_, Vol. 6, No. 1, Winter 2005,
pp. 3–8. Per the editor's note in that issue, the result became a standard
interview question at RiskMetrics.
