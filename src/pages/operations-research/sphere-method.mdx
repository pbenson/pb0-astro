# The ball that rolls downhill

<PlainWords>
A linear program is a room whose walls are flat, and a direction you want to
travel as far as possible. The classic method walks the corners of the room,
testing one after another. That works, but corners are where the arithmetic is
worst behaved, and a room in a thousand dimensions has an enormous number of
them.

The method here stays in the middle instead. At any point, ask for the largest
ball that fits without poking through a wall. Where the ball touches tells you
which walls are close; the lowest point of the ball tells you which way is
downhill. Move, grow a new ball, repeat.
</PlainWords>

Take the linear program

$$
\min\; c \cdot x \quad \text{subject to} \quad Ax \ge b
$$

and stay strictly inside the feasible region $K$ the whole way. No pivoting, no
basis, and — the claim this page is really about — no matrix inversion.

## The largest ball that fits

For a point $x$ inside $K$, write $\delta(x)$ for the radius of the largest ball
centred there that still fits inside:

$$
\delta(x) = \min_i \frac{A_i \cdot x - b_i}{\lVert A_i \rVert}
$$

That is a minimum over the constraints of a distance — the perpendicular
distance from $x$ to each facet. No system is solved to get it, which is the
first place the usual machinery is absent.

Call that ball $B(x)$. The constraints achieving the minimum are the **touching
set** $T(x)$: the facets $B(x)$ actually rests against. Usually one. At a point
equidistant from several facets, more — and the set jumps as $x$ moves, which is
worth watching rather than hiding.

Each touching constraint gives a **touching point** $x^i$, the orthogonal
projection of the centre onto that facet. And sliding the objective plane down
the $-c$ direction until it first meets the ball gives the **bottom point**: the
best point of the ball, by objective value.

## What ε is for

A descent step runs to the boundary, and the boundary is exactly where the
method must not go: on a facet, $\delta = 0$, the ball vanishes, and there is
nothing left to compute a direction from. So each step stops short, at the
**near touching point**

$$
(1 - \varepsilon)\, x^i + \varepsilon\, x
$$

a fraction of the way back toward the centre. Turn ε down and the steps hug the
walls; turn it up and they stay timid and central.

## Why the iterate wanders

Watch the path and you will see it move *away* from the objective sometimes.
That is the centering step, and it is deliberate: a bigger ball gives the next
descent step more room to run. The method's answer is therefore not where it
currently stands but the best point it has seen — the ring, not the dot.

## The argument

Standard LP codes solve a linear system at every step, over and over. That is
what makes dense models expensive, and it is where floating-point error
accumulates. Everything on this page is a dot product, a norm and a minimum:

- $\delta(x)$ is a minimum of $m$ distances.
- $T(x)$ is the arg-min of the same list.
- A touching point is one projection, $x - A_i (A_i \cdot x - b_i)/\lVert A_i \rVert^2$.
- A step length is a ratio of two dot products.

No factorisation, no inverse, nothing to become ill-conditioned. In two
dimensions that is a curiosity. The reason it matters is that none of it gets
harder in a thousand.

## Notes

The picture is a port of a p5.js sketch of mine from January 2023, which drew
one ball and one step; the iteration, the path and the incumbent are new here.

The centering step is a plain hill-climb on $\delta$ — step along the sum of the
outward normals of $T(x)$, backtracking until the ball actually grows. That is
not the paper's centering procedure, which does more; it is enough to show what
centering is *for*.
